# Narrative techniques — playbook

Last updated: 2026-08-12

Creative techniques and format ideas for the narrative stage (see [pipeline.md](../pipeline.md), stage 4). This is a pool to draw from, not a checklist — using every technique in every video would itself become a template, which is exactly what the monetization policy penalizes (see [research/youtube-monetization-policy.md](../research/youtube-monetization-policy.md)).

## Character-driven format (undecided as a default, in active use)

Rather than a straight documentary-style summary of a topic, a video can follow a character moving through real historical events or settings, working toward or accomplishing something, or meeting a dramatic fate. This maps closely onto YouTube's own policy language for what's monetizable — "using AI to visualize a unique character and narrative you invented" — and a distinct character/premise per video is a strong, legible way to satisfy "materially varied" content across a channel.

Open question: how a character-driven device interacts with the fact-checking pipeline. If the character is a real historical figure, the normal accuracy bar applies directly. If the character is fictional or a composite used to dramatize real events, that needs honest framing in the video itself (e.g., "a composite character based on real accounts") so it doesn't blur into presenting invented specifics as documented fact.

## Unexplained human detail

AI-generated video has a natural tendency toward a slightly surreal, gap-filled quality — this can read as off-putting, but it can also be an asset: leaving some detail slightly unresolved invites the viewer's imagination to fill it in, rather than everything being explicitly set up and paid off. Concretely: give a character one subtle, human, *unexplained* habit or tic — something a viewer notices and wonders about rather than something narrated or resolved.

Examples explored so far: a private counting habit, an unconscious habit with a piece of gear, a limp or physical habit with no stated backstory.

First applied instance: the D-Day dry run (see [../dry-runs/dday-omaha-beach.md](../dry-runs/dday-omaha-beach.md)) used a private, unexplained counting habit under the soldier's breath, which breaks and restarts at moments of shock.

### Variant: tic continuity across a cut

Specific to continuous/countable tics, not tics in general: occasionally let the detail bridge a scene cut to imply elapsed time — e.g., a count left at "...2...3..." at the end of one scene picked back up mid-count ("...30...31...") at the start of the next, implying time has passed without narrating it. Should be rare, not a recurring device. Doesn't generalize to every kind of tic — it works because counting is inherently sequential and legible across a cut, unlike, say, a limp, which doesn't carry "time has passed" information.

## "Walk through history" (future video type, deferred)

A character walking through a piece of history, with the environment/setting changing around them to show progression over time — its own video type, leaning more educational than the character-driven format above, but not fundamentally different in mechanism from the D-Day dry run. Not interactive or navigable — that was a mistake in an earlier draft of the project's vision doc, not the actual intent. Deliberately not started yet, per the decision to go deep on one direction (D-Day-style) before reconsidering.

## POV is configurable, not assumed

First-person vs. third-person shouldn't be hardcoded anywhere in the pipeline — different scenarios call for different POV, and the choice is made per video at the narrative stage, the same way audio format is (see pipeline.md, stage 10). Applies to "walk through history" as much as to any other format.
